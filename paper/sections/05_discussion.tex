\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Interpreting the A $>$ B $>$ C ordering.}
% Source: analysis/results.md, analysis/error_taxonomy.md, analysis/token_sweep_compare.md
System~A outperforms both structured variants on exact-match accuracy by 5.3~pp (vs.\ B)
and 7.2~pp (vs.\ C), with statistically significant pairwise differences.  The error
taxonomy attributes 44--58\% of failures to reasoning truncation under the 512-token
output budget, raising the question of whether the ordering is conditional on that
budget.  The 1024-token sensitivity sweep in \S\ref{sec:results:budget_sweep}
answers this directly: doubling the answer budget gives no benefit to System~A
($\Delta = 0.000$), but moves System~B by $+3.8$~pp and System~C by $+5.1$~pp on
the same paired sample.  C gains slightly more than B, consistent with C's superior
retrieval recall (0.852 vs.\ 0.762 at $k=5$; Table~\ref{tab:recall}) being partly
masked by truncation in the 512-token regime.

The budget effect is real and quantifiable, but it does not flip the ordering.
At 1024 tokens, A still leads C by 6.0~pp and B by 5.2~pp.  The A $>$ B $>$ C
ordering is therefore not an artefact of the 512-token cap alone, even though
that cap accounts for roughly half of the structured-system deficit.  A practitioner
choosing between architectures should expect the gap to narrow but not close as the
output budget grows.

The no-retrieval baseline (\S\ref{sec:results:noretrieval}) shows that
$25.2\%$ of overall accuracy is question-text-attributable: qwen3-32b answers
this fraction of questions correctly with no patient context at all, drawing on
prior knowledge of typical specialty-medication dosing patterns and on the
prevalence of canonical responses in the question phrasing.  The remaining
accuracy is genuinely retrieval-attributable: A adds $+15.4$~pp on top of the
floor, B adds $+10.1$~pp, and C adds $+8.2$~pp; all three paired bootstrap CIs
exclude zero.  The retrieval-attributable component preserves the A $>$ B $>$ C
ordering, indicating that the QA win of System A is not driven by a higher
question-text-attributable floor but by how much each retrieval substrate adds to
the LLM's prior.

\paragraph{Tier 3: retriever vs answer LLM.}
% Source: analysis/tier3_recall_breakdown.md (run_tier3_recall_breakdown.py)
All three systems sit below the per-family modal baseline at Tier~3
(\S\ref{sec:results:tiers}, 19.8--20.4\% vs.\ 24.8\%).  This raises the question of
whether the failure is upstream (retriever missing relevant resources) or downstream
(answer LLM unable to reason over the retrieved evidence).  At Tier~3, retrieval
recall@5 remains high across four of the five families for both structured systems:
\texttt{cross\_resource} 89.7\% / 99.7\%, \texttt{regimen\_aggregation} 83.2\% /
94.1\%, \texttt{regimen\_compliance} 76.6\% / 89.9\%, and \texttt{temporal\_comparison}
80.0\% / 77.4\% for B and C respectively.  The supporting evidence is in the
retrieved context on most Tier-3 questions yet the answer LLM still produces wrong
answers, so the Tier-3 collapse is a reasoning bottleneck, not a retrieval bottleneck.
The Tier-3 $+13$~pp lift from retrieval (\S\ref{sec:results:noretrieval}) confirms
the retrieved context carries real signal; the reason this signal does not translate
into a clearance of the modal baseline is that the answer LLM cannot reliably
synthesise it under multi-component regimens.
The 1024-token sweep moves Tier-3 by $+3$--$5$~pp but does not clear the modal
baseline, indicating that longer outputs alone are insufficient.  The architectural
implication is that multi-component regimens may require structured intermediate
steps -- per-component decomposition, explicit date enumeration before aggregation,
or chain-of-thought scaffolding -- rather than additional one-shot reasoning budget.
\texttt{temporal\_lookup} is the one family where System~C does not beat System~B at
Tier-3 recall (76.5\% vs.\ 78.0\%); a follow-up should examine whether C's temporal
pre-filter is over-pruning candidate \texttt{Observation} or
\texttt{MedicationAdministration} resources at the highest complexity tier.

\paragraph{Why the narrative format has a temporal weakness.}
The temporal-anchor failure rate gradient (A:~30\%, B:~18\%, C:~10\%) is the most
structurally informative finding in the error taxonomy.  Narrative chunks do not
contain the question's \texttt{reference\_date} (a per-question parameter that changes
across rows in the question bank), so the LLM must infer a temporal anchor from
surrounding text.  FHIR resources carry explicit date fields
(CarePlan.period.start/end, MedicationAdministration.effectiveDateTime,
MedicationRequest.authoredOn) that enable the LLM to reason about ``when'' even when
the reference date is absent from the prompt context.  This is a structural property
that would persist regardless of token budget, and it motivates future work embedding
reference-date context directly into narrative chunks at indexing time.

\paragraph{Format vs.\ LLM lexical regularity.}
The templated-narrative ablation (\S\ref{sec:results:templated}) addresses a
natural objection: System~A's advantage might be specific to LLM-generated
text, in which case the result would not transfer to non-LLM narrative sources.
A-T uses a deterministic template generator with no LLM and matches the
LLM-narrative arm overall ($+0.74$~pp [$+0.13$, $+1.34$]).  This result
supports two co-equal conclusions.  First, narrative \emph{format} is the
load-bearing property, not LLM-specific lexical regularity.  Second, narrative
\emph{style} matters within the narrative format: templated narratives win by
$+10.21$~pp on the date-arithmetic family \texttt{temporal\_comparison}
(where explicit dose-date tables help) and lose by $-7.29$~pp on the
dose-aggregation family \texttt{regimen\_aggregation} (where LLM prose
summaries help); neither single-style narrative is uniformly superior.  We
sketch a hybrid-narrative direction in \S\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\paragraph{The cross-resource family as a scope statement.}
Structured retrieval should, in theory, have the largest advantage on cross-resource
questions that require traversing FHIR references (e.g.\ linking a CarePlan to its
MedicationRequests to their Medications).  In practice, cross-resource accuracy is at
parity across all three systems (A=43.4\%, B=44.5\%, C=43.4\%;
Table~\ref{tab:family_results}).  The retained \texttt{cross\_resource} templates target
tier label, schedule kind, and component presence: each of these resolves a single FHIR
reference rather than a multi-hop traversal.  The current question bank therefore does
not exercise multi-hop reference traversal, and we do not claim evidence for or against
a structural advantage of resource-aware retrieval on harder cross-resource templates.
Whether such an advantage emerges under multi-hop traversal questions is a scope
question for follow-up work, not a methodological miss in this evaluation.

\paragraph{The representation dissociation: QA winner $\neq$ ML-feature winner.}
The central finding of the feature-extraction arm is a \emph{dissociation} between
QA performance and adherence-prediction performance.  System~A wins the QA comparison
(40.6\% vs.\ 33.4--35.3\%), but FS-Structured features substantially outperform
FS-Narrative features on adherence classification (LightGBM class-weighted AUC 0.997
vs.\ 0.846; bootstrap CI excludes zero).  FS-Narrative and FS-Aware are tied within
the bootstrap interval ($\Delta{=}+0.071$ [$-0.039$, $+0.190$]).

The practical implication is that the choice of representation should be made
\emph{separately} for each downstream task.  A clinical workflow that uses narrative RAG
for case-manager Q\&A (where A wins) and structured FHIR features for an adherence
risk-stratification model (where FS-Structured wins) would outperform a workflow that
uses a single representation for both.  This finding complements prior work by Nateghi
Haredasht et al.~\citep{nateghi2025predicting} (LLM-extracted narrative features
for MOUD retention) and Gao et al.~\citep{gao2024raw} (raw numeric features
often match or beat LLM embeddings), which together establish that neither representation
uniformly dominates across downstream use cases.

\paragraph{A point where readers may disagree.}
A reasonable reviewer might argue that the near-perfect FS-Structured AUC (0.997--1.000)
renders the feature-arm comparison trivial: if structured features are essentially
tautological summaries of the labelling rule, the ``dissociation'' finding collapses to
``the features used to generate the label also predict the label.''  We acknowledge this
directly.  The synthetic-label design (consecutive missed doses OR gap $>$ 1.5$\times$
interval) was chosen for reproducibility and programmatic verifiability; its alignment
with the FS-Structured feature set is intentional and documented.  The meaningful signal
is not the absolute FS-Structured AUC, but (1) the tied CI between FS-Narrative and FS-Aware
despite very different feature sources, and (2) the relative ordering (Structured $\gg$
Narrative $\approx$ Aware) which is robust across both classifiers.  We do not claim
that structured features will achieve near-perfect AUC on a real noisy cohort; we claim
that they encode the adherence signal more directly than narrative-derived or
retrieval-trace features, a claim that should hold under richer real-world labels as well.

A non-tautological remediation -- replacing the synthetic label with a Synthea-derived
clinical-outcome label (e.g., a disease-progression encounter, treatment switch, or
hospitalisation event post-regimen-start) -- was investigated before submission and is
not feasible on the current cohort.  The synthetic bundles do not contain post-regimen
disease-progression events of the kind that would yield such a label; in a representative
positive Tier-2 case there is exactly one encounter post-medication-start and no condition
or observation that could plausibly proxy a non-adherence outcome.  Constructing such a
label requires either regenerating the Synthea data with regimen-impact modules or moving
to a real PSP cohort, both of which are deferred to full-venue follow-up work.

\paragraph{Practical guidance for system designers.}
For practitioners building RAG over specialty-medication clinical records, the choice of
representation should follow the downstream task.  When the answer LLM's output budget
is constrained, narrative RAG is the lower-risk choice: its compact context (490 mean
tokens-in vs.\ 4,463 for System~B) allows more reasoning headroom within a fixed token
limit.  If output budgets are generous, structured RAG closes part of the gap but does
not overtake narrative RAG on QA accuracy in our evaluation: the 1024-token sweep
recovers $+5.1$~pp for System~C and $+3.8$~pp for System~B while leaving System~A
unchanged, narrowing but not eliminating A's lead (\S\ref{sec:results:budget_sweep}).
For downstream adherence risk stratification, structured FHIR features are preferable
to reusing the narrative representation: extracting numeric adherence metrics directly
from FHIR resources is both more interpretable and more predictive in this evaluation.
Finally, LLM-generated narratives should be treated as summaries, not as drop-in
replacements for the structured record.  The fidelity audit confirms 100\% entity
recovery after prompt iteration, but entity recovery does not guarantee that every
temporal relation is preserved in a form the answer LLM can exploit during reasoning.
